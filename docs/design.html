01Built

Godot, and why the engine barely matters

Turn-based tactics puts almost no load on a renderer or a physics solver, which is where Unity and Unreal earn their keep. Godot 4 is MIT-licensed, starts fast, iterates fast, and exports to all three desktop platforms from .NET. Desktop-only also erases its one real C# limitation, which is web export.

Unity does win on two things that matter for this game specifically: the asset store, and humanoid animation retargeting. Realistic soldiers with cover transitions and credible weapon handling are much cheaper to build there. That risk is real, and it arrives late — a year of systems work before it bites.

So the engine decision is deliberately made cheap to reverse. The simulation is a plain C# class library with zero engine references. Hex geometry, cover, movement, line of sight, detection, initiative, damage and AI all live in Hexcom.Core. Godot reads that state and draws it.

  • The rules are unit-testable headless — 259 tests run in about a second today.
  • Balance can be tuned by running thousands of AI-vs-AI matches with no window open.
  • If the art pipeline ever forces a move, only the view layer is lost.

This constraint is worth more than the engine choice, and it is what makes the engine choice safe. The rule to enforce forever: nothing under src/ may say using Godot;.

02Built

The grid, and the corner graph under it

Axial coordinates with cube maths, flat-top orientation. Flat-top is baked into the logical model because the corner-naming scheme below depends on orientation; the view can still render pointy-top by rotating the layout 30°, and the rules never notice.

The part that earns its complexity is corner identity. A hex has six corners, each shared by three hexes — so two corners per hex, and every corner in the plane is the due-east corner of exactly one hex or the due-west corner of exactly one hex. Naming them that way gives each corner a single canonical identity no matter which hex you ask through, which is what lets walls live on one global corner graph instead of being duplicated per tile.

Each corner connects to twelve others: three along hex sides, and nine interior chords, three in each of the three hexes it touches.

03Built

Cover is a chord, not a tile property

Because a hex's six corners each connect to the other five, a single hex can carry fifteen distinct wall segments. That is the entire cover vocabulary. A building face, a sandbag line clipping one corner, and a barricade running diagonally across a courtyard are all the same kind of object, differing only in which two corners they join.

Regions, and the transit rule

Chords cut a hex into regions, computed by walking the faces of the small planar graph the chords form. A region needs 60% of a hex before a unit can stand in it, which puts the natural cut sizes cleanly on either side of the line: 5/6 qualifies, 1/2 does not.

Sub-60% regions become transit nodes — pathed through, never valid as a destination. A wall bisecting a tile is therefore crossable and unoccupiable, exactly as intended, and the one-unit-per-tile invariant survives intact. No special case is written anywhere; it falls out of the area threshold.

Determining cover

Because walls are real segments in world space, cover is a geometry query rather than a lookup table: trace the line from shooter to target, collect the segments it crosses, take the most protective. A sandbag clipping one corner protects across a narrow arc; a building face protects across a wide one. This is strictly better than the usual six cover flags per tile, and it is the same code for every wall in the game.

04Built

Combat

Fifty points a turn

Two actions in the XCOM style cannot price a ladder honestly against a footstep. A stride costs five and a turn is fifty, so ten hexes of open ground is a whole turn spent doing nothing else. Firing costs about half a turn, so the archetypal turn is move a little, shoot, take cover — and the interesting decisions are all in what you give up to do the third thing.

It started at ten, with a stride at one, and that turned out to be the wrong number for a reason worth recording: the cheapest action sets the resolution of everything else. Nothing could be priced below a stride, so turning on the spot cost a full step; and no per-soldier multiplier could make anybody quicker over open ground, only slower, because one point cannot be reduced. At five a turn is worth two, going flat is worth three strides a hex, and a multiplier anywhere from three fifths to double lands on a distinct number.

It matters twice over, because action points are also the clock a reaction window runs on. A ten hex run is fifty ticks long rather than ten, so placing a shot to land just as somebody clears a wall is a real choice rather than a rounding accident. Every ratio survived the change untouched — the whole price list was multiplied by five — so this cost nothing but the arithmetic in the tests.

Unspent points convert to reaction potential rather than evaporating. That single rule ties the economy to the stealth game: hanging back with points in hand is how you cover an approach, and sprinting is how you arrive somewhere with nothing left to answer with.

Firing costs: snap shot 15, standard 25, aimed 35. Change stance 2, turn on the spot 2, declare an overwatch arc 5. Reloading is specified but not built. Seven tenths of whatever is left over becomes reaction potential — see section 09 for why that fraction.

05Built

Movement is a graph of priced links

Not a grid of equal steps. Every connection between two places carries its own type and its own price, so a ladder eating most of a turn needs no special case anywhere — the pathfinder is plain Dijkstra over action points, and "how far can this unit get" and "how do I get there" are the same query.

Climbs, ledges and drops are generated automatically from floor heights. Ladders, stairs, doors and ziplines are authored. Terrain surcharges come from the ground type.

06Built

One trace, two answers

Can I see you, and how exposed are you. Those are the same question asked at two heights, so they come out of one trace. The sight line is flattened onto the ground and intersected with the wall segments it crosses. At each crossing the top of the wall is projected back along the line onto the target as a waterline: everything below it is hidden, everything above it can be seen and shot.

Cover is graded by how much of the silhouette falls below that line. The target is invisible when an opaque wall submerges all of it. That one construction replaces the usual pile of special cases.

What comes free

None of the following is written down anywhere in the rules. Each is what the geometry already says, which is the test that the model is the right one.

  • Stance. A standing soldier is visible over waist-high cover. The same soldier prone behind it is gone.
  • Range. A low wall far down the line hides nothing, because the waterline it projects is below the target's feet by then.
  • Elevation. High ground cancels low cover on its own, and leaves head-high cover mostly intact.
  • Point blank. An attacker in the next hex shoots down over the wall their target is hugging, so cover drops to almost nothing.
  • Storeys. A wall projects a band, not just a line, so a raised walkway can be seen underneath.

Materials describe matter, not situations

A wall profile says whether light passes through it and the best grade it can ever give. It does not say whether you can see over it — that is the trace's job. So sandbags are opaque and capped at half cover; chain link is transparent and capped at light, however much of you is behind it; smoke is opaque, capped at light, and you can walk straight through it.

Bug found by a test

A silhouette exactly as tall as the wall hiding it produces a hidden share of 0.9999999999999999, which read as a sliver of head showing over the top. A soldier was visible through a solid building wall. Fixed with an epsilon, and the boundary now has a test of its own.

What it hands the interface

Every trace returns the wall responsible for the grade, so the interface can point at the actual sandbag instead of showing an abstract shield, and an exposure figure from zero to one that will drive hit chance once shooting exists. Both are symmetric and side-agnostic, because the detection model below runs the same query in the enemy's direction, and the AI must be restricted to exactly what the interface can show you.

07Built

Seeing first

The thing to design away from is the aggro pull — you open on two guards and find yourself fighting seven, because proximity to a trigger volume told a group of enemies about you instantly and for free. The fix is to make alertness travel as information rather than as a radius. Every enemy that knows about you learned it somewhere, through a channel the player can see and cut.

Awareness is a state, held per enemy

UnawarePatrol behaviour. No idea you exist.
SuspiciousSomething registered. Stops, scans, does not commit.

Detection accumulates

No binary spotted check. Each enemy builds detection against each of your units per turn, from distance, cover and concealment, your stance, how many points you spent moving, the noise you made, light level, their perception, and whether you are inside their facing cone. Crossing a threshold advances the state one rung; silence lets it decay.

Noise is its own channel with its own propagation — ground type has a noise factor already, gravel is loud and grass is quiet — and noise produces an investigate goal at a location, not a global alert.

Alerts propagate through channels you can cut

  • Line of sight — an enemy who sees an alerted comrade takes on some of what they know.
  • Voice — short range, blocked by walls.
  • Radio — map-wide, but it belongs to specific people. Killing the radio operator quietly is a tactic, and target prioritisation becomes an information decision rather than a damage one.

The window

Looking happens on the observer's own turn, not continuously. A sentry that has already acted this round will not notice you until it comes round again — so the turn order strip is not just a readout, it is a tool. Reading it to find the gap and crossing the open ground inside it is meant to be a real decision.

Hearing does not wait. Move loudly and everyone in earshot knows something happened immediately, which is what stops the window from being a free pass.

Asymmetric information, deliberately

The player reads their own soldiers' exposure as an exact figure, because that is information about themselves and hiding it would be fog rather than tension. What an enemy believes comes back coarse: a rung on the ladder above, a marker for where they last had contact, and how many rounds stale it is. An exact certainty number would turn the approach into arithmetic.

That split is enforced in the code rather than left to discipline. The full record, with the certainty figure, is what the rules and the AI read. The interface goes through a separate coarse readout type that has no number on it.

The last-known-position marker is the payoff: it is where an enemy thinks you are, and it goes stale the moment you move. The gap between the marker and the truth is the thing the stealth game is actually played in.

Facing, so a position can be flanked

A unit looks one way. Inside a hundred and twenty degrees it notices things at full rate; out to two hundred it catches them in the corner of an eye; behind, at a twelfth. Coming at a sentry from the rear is worth an order of magnitude, which is what makes the long way round a real option rather than flavour.

Facing decides how readily something is noticed, never whether it could be seen at all. Line of sight stays pure geometry in the sight solver; who is paying attention to what is a question about people, and it lives in the awareness model. Moving turns you to face your line of travel for nothing; watching one way while standing still costs a point, and that is the whole decision.

Carrying yourself low is priced, not free. A crouch costs three fifths again on every traversal and going flat costs three times, so a crawler covers three hexes where an upright soldier covers ten. That is the whole trade the stealth game runs on: prone you are harder to see, harder to notice, quieter and lower than most cover, and the bill is the ground you do not cross. Until it was charged for there was no reason ever to stand back up.

The trap, and it was live for a while, is pricing noise off what a move cost. Crawling is three times the price, so a crawler spending three times the points to cross one hex came out very nearly as loud as somebody strolling over it — the stance cancelling itself out and the reason to go flat with it. Effort is a property of the ground crossed and how it was crossed, not of the bill; a slow soldier is not a noisy one.

Still to come

Attacking from concealment giving a damage and accuracy bonus, on top of the initiative payoff in the next section. Noise currently ignores walls entirely — you hear people through a building, which is right, but a closed door should count for something.

08Built

Initiative, and the ambush it breaks

Individual interleaved initiative in the D&D sense: one of yours, two of theirs, one of yours, and so on, ordered by a stat plus a roll, modified by gear weight. Rerolled every round so the order does not calcify.

What ships is a queue over a battle clock rather than a list of sides taking it in turns. A round is a thousand ticks wide; initiative is a rating plus a ten-sided roll less encumbrance, and a higher result buys a place earlier in the round. Rebooking happens the moment a unit becomes active rather than when its turn ends, so the queue always holds one future turn per unit and the order strip can look ahead — which the player needs, because deciding whether to move now or hold depends on who acts next.

Every roll comes from one seeded generator owned by the battle. A whole fight replays identically from a seed and a list of commands, which is the property the headless balance runs depend on.

The tension worth naming

Interleaved initiative and stealth pull against each other. XCOM ambushes feel good precisely because concealment breaks and all six of your units fire before anyone reacts. Interleaving destroys that: the enemy acts between your shots, and a coordinated opening becomes impossible to execute.

The fix is an explicit Ambush action. A unit spends points to hold a shot against an arc. When any ambushing unit fires — by choice, or because they were spotted — every other unit holding on that target resolves in the same window, before the enemy acts. Alpha strikes survive; the turn structure does not need a special case; and the AP economy already produces the resource it spends, because held points were going to become reaction potential anyway.

Undetected units also take a large initiative bonus in the first round after contact, which is the mechanical payoff for having played the approach well.

Open  The clock is in place, so moving to a continuous time-unit system — the Battle Brothers or Final Fantasy Tactics model, where a cheap action brings you round sooner — means replacing the round arithmetic in one method with actAt += cost. Nothing else changes. Whether that model is better here is still unresolved: it is deeper, and it is harder to read at a glance. The decision can wait until there is enough game to feel the difference.

09Built

Three ways to act out of turn

Reactions all draw on the same resource: action points left over at the end of your turn. That closes the loop on the economy — sprinting somewhere leaves you with nothing to answer with, and hanging back with points in hand is how you cover an approach. The choice to reserve is made before you know whether it will pay, which is the right shape for a decision.

How much of the leftover actually carries is a difficulty setting, and it applies to both sides. Turned up, the whole battlefield is twitchier: every mistake gets answered and committing to a move becomes frightening. Turned down, turns feel more like turns. It changes the texture of the game rather than handing the player a handicap.

The dial settled at seven tenths. It is a share rather than a fixed number, which matters more than the number does: the ten point turn is provisional, and the allowance is expected to vary by soldier once stats, training and kit feed into it, so nothing here may assume ten. What is calibrated is the shape — seven tenths puts the three rungs of the movement economy on either side of the fire mode prices, so each is a real decision rather than a rounding accident. Against a ten point turn it lands exactly: hold everything and seven bank, which is an aimed shot; spend half the turn moving and three bank, which is a snap shot; sprint and nothing banks. A floor cuts anything below two points, because a leftover nothing can be spent on is noise and offering it is worse than rounding it away.

The price list is not the same thing as what a given soldier pays for it. A ladder is a ladder and a snap shot is a snap shot — that is a fact about the world — but what this trooper spends climbing one or getting one off is about them, their training and their kit, and it lives on the unit as a pair of multipliers. So a scout who is quick over ground and slow to bring a weapon to bear, and a gunner who is the other way round, spend the same fifty points on entirely different turns. A stabilised mount that takes a standard shot from five points to four is a multiplier on the wearer, not a change to what a standard shot is.

Pricing is applied when a route is costed rather than baked into the movement graph. The graph describes the ground and is built once for the map; rebuilding it per soldier to say that one of them is slow would be absurd. And because cost is time inside a reaction window, none of this is only economy: a watchman slow on the trigger places its shot later on the mover's timeline and catches them further along the route. Being the slow one has a consequence measured in ticks.

Open

Two things to settle when the allowance stops being a flat ten. First, prices are absolute while the allowance is not, so a soldier given more points does not merely move further — they bank a larger reserve and afford better shots out of it, and the advantage compounds across three systems at once. Second, the floor of one point per action is where ten runs out of resolution: a walk already costs one, so no multiplier can make a soldier meaningfully quicker over open ground, only slower. Both point at the same answer — a bigger turn — and both should be decided together rather than guessed at now.

Keeping it from becoming a cascade

The failure mode is obvious enough to design against up front: every action triggering a volley of responses, each of which is itself an action, and a turn taking five minutes to resolve. Three things hold it back, and only the last is a rule.

  • The economy does most of the work. Reactions are meant to be a feature of the approach, not of the firefight. Move slowly and methodically and the bank is real; once the shooting starts almost everything gets spent, and what is left is usually below the price of even a snap shot.
  • A reserve is spent, not refreshed. Points used reacting are gone until that unit's next turn, so a unit reacts about once a round however many chances it gets. Nobody answers the same move twice.
  • Only units that could actually act are asked. Reserve in hand, the mover crossing their arc at some point in the window, and a shot they can afford — that is a short list most of the time, and usually an empty one mid-firefight.

In practice the third one does most of the visible work. Building the offer list for a window means asking every hostile whether it holds an arc, has anything banked, can afford a shot, and would have the mover in view at some tick — and the answer is usually no for everyone, so most moves open a window that resolves to nothing at all.

Ambush

The answer to the problem in section 08. A squad arms against an agreed trigger and waits; when one of them fires, every armed unit resolves in that same window before the enemy acts. Alpha strikes survive interleaved initiative, and the turn structure needs no special case — the reserved points were already going to become reaction potential.

Arming costs points up front on top of the shot held back, so setting an ambush is a turn spent not advancing — for every member of it. Spring it early and the coordination is wasted; wait too long and somebody sees you first.

Structurally it is the one reaction that needed something new, and it turned out to need less than expected. A window is built on a committed move, and an ambush has no mover: the target is standing still. So it gets a route of no length — one instant, the same timeline — and everything else follows. Placements still land in the order of what they cost, which means the snap shots go before the aimed ones, and if the cheap ones put the target down the expensive ones are never taken and those reserves are never spent.

There are two ways to be the one who says now. The member taking a turn can choose the moment deliberately, and pays out of that turn while everybody else pays out of the reserves they banked when they armed. Or somebody simply walks into an armed arc, in which case nobody is having a turn and the whole squad answers out of reserve. Both open the same window; the difference is only which pocket the first shot comes from.

Springing calls the contact in before anybody fires, which is what makes the trap a squad action rather than a coincidence. An ambusher has to be told — by radio, by shout, or by having eyes on the target already — or it does not fire, so the radio operator is back at the centre of things and an ambusher posted somewhere nobody can reach is decoration. And because everybody but the springer pays out of a reserve, an ambusher who got bored and spent its turn going somewhere is still armed, still looking the right way, and out of the ambush.

Surprise

The only involuntary one, and the only one nobody has to plan for. When a unit suddenly registers an opponent it may spend part of what it has left, immediately: turn to face, fire, drop into cover, or shout.

Less of the reserve is available than for a deliberate reaction, because reacting is not planning — half of it, and no aiming bonus. The point is that ambushing a competent soldier should not be free. Walking round the back of a sentry deserves a reward; catching one with nothing at all in hand ought to take more than proximity.

The trigger is a crossing, and getting that right is most of what makes surprise work rather than flood the game. Not "can see them" — a soldier already being tracked is not a surprise however plainly they walk past. Not "learned something" either, or every step of an opponent climbing your awareness ladder would startle you again. It is the single moment a contact goes from below the bar to above it: once per opponent, and never once the shooting has started and everybody already knows.

Two bars, not one, because what you may do depends on how sure you are. Something registering at the edge of what you can make out is enough to duck or spin round; it is nowhere near enough to shoot at, and a soldier who fires at every half-seen shape is not a competent one. So a crawler at forty metres will make a sentry twitch and will not draw fire for it — which is exactly the outcome the careful approach is played for.

It reaches wider than an overwatch does, out to the whole arc a unit can see into at all rather than the slice it is properly attending to. Catching something in the corner of an eye is the case being modelled, and it is the only one where turning to face it is worth an action. Behind is still behind: walk round the back and nothing flinches.

The beat before a surprise starts is where "the weapon was already pointed" becomes mechanical rather than a claim. Without it, a sentry with nothing declared answers exactly as fast as one that spent a point and its whole turn watching the right ground, and overwatch is left buying only the bonus. With it, the same soldier firing the same snap shot lands later and catches the runner further along a route it is already committed to.

There is one more thing the reserve turns out to be doing, and it was not designed in so much as noticed afterwards. A reactor gets its look at the crossing only if it has points banked — so the reserve is alertness as well as reaction potential. A soldier who spent the lot is head down and busy and does not clock you going past. Hanging back keeps your eyes up as well as your options open, which is the same decision the whole economy is built around.

Overwatch

A watchman declares an arc and holds it. Anything moving inside is shot at, at a bonus, because the weapon was already pointed there. A narrower arc aims better, so the decision is how much ground to try to cover against how well.

This is where facing earns its keep twice: the arc a unit is watching is the same arc it notices things in, so an overwatch position is also a detection position — and flanking it defeats both at once.

The bonus multiplies rather than adds, so overwatching pays best on a shot that was plausible anyway and never turns a hopeless one into a good one. Declaring costs a single point out of the turn, plus the usual price of turning if the arc is not where the unit was already looking. That one point matters more than it looks: it means a watchman banks six, so an overwatch shot is never an aimed one. Overwatch pays in the aim bonus instead of in aiming time, and a narrow arc is worth roughly what taking your time is worth — at snap shot speed. That is the whole reason it is strong.

A watchman only fires at somebody it has actually noticed. Geometry alone is not enough: without that gate, a sentry blazes away at a prone crawler forty metres out with four per cent of itself showing — someone the detection model says it has not registered at all — and overwatch becomes the one place in the game where something happens to a hidden soldier without the awareness ladder being consulted.

The gate needs a companion to be worth anything, though, and it is worth naming why. Looking normally happens only on your own turn, so a soldier who was behind a building when the sentry last looked could step out into a declared arc and cross it untouched — the canonical overwatch situation, defeated by the turn order. So holding an arc is looking down it: movement inside gets a proper detection check at the tick it happens, through the ordinary model. Stance, cover, range, exposure and perception all still apply, so a careful approach is as invisible to a watchman as to anybody else, and a jog across the open is not. That extra look outside your own turn is precisely what the point and the held reserve bought.

Arc edges are exclusive, and decided rather than left to arithmetic. Hex bearings are exact multiples of sixty degrees, so a place sitting precisely on the edge of a hundred and twenty degree arc is the common case and not a rare one — the maths that gets there lands on 60.00000000000001 as readily as on 60. The edge belongs to the wider arc, in declared overwatches and in attention cones alike. A standard arc held toward one spoke therefore does not reach the next spoke over: cover that ground and you declare a wider arc, and shoot worse down all of it.

Action points are time, inside the window

A reaction does not resolve before or after the move that triggered it. It resolves during it, on a shared clock, and the currency of that clock is action points.

The move that triggered the window is already committed, so both sides know the future for its duration. A five point run is five ticks long, and the route is known: at tick three the mover is three hexes along, wherever that is. A reactor spending from its reserve places its actions on that same timeline. Whatever it starts at tick t and costs k resolves at t plus k — against wherever the mover will be by then, not where it is now.

The standard shot is the better shot and it misses the moment: it goes off at tick twenty-five, by which time the target has arrived and is behind cover. The snap shot is worse in every respect except the one that matters here — it lands at tick fifteen, while the target is still two hexes short of the wall, and leaves ten points to duck away afterwards. This is what a watchman actually chooses, unprompted: the recommendation weighs expected damage against where the runner will be, and picks the cheap shot the moment the destination has cover.

This is what makes cheap actions worth having. A snap shot that is simply a worse aimed shot is never chosen; a snap shot that is the only one that connects is a real decision. The same arithmetic explains why overwatch is strong: the weapon is already pointed, so the shot is cheap, so it lands early in the window while the target is still in the open.

It gives the mover something to think about too. A long run through open ground hands every reactor a wide window; a two point shuffle hands them almost nothing but barely gets anywhere. How far to commit becomes the question, rather than simply where to end up.

Structurally this costs less than it sounds. A route already arrives as a list of links with an action point price on each, so "where is the mover at cumulative cost three" is arithmetic on data that exists. What is new is a window object that holds the committed route, collects each reactor's placements, and resolves them in tick order.

It turned out cheaper still than that, because of one decision worth recording: resolving a window actually walks the mover along its route, stopping at each tick something lands and firing from there. The mover is genuinely standing where the timeline says it is, so line of sight, cover grade, range band and which plate the round arrives at all come back from the same code that answers them on anybody's turn. There is no hypothetical-position path through the shooting rules and no second version of the geometry to keep honest. A runner dropped three ticks into a four tick run simply stops there, one hex short of the wall it was running for.

Only the ticks at which the mover arrives somewhere are worth considering — in between, nothing about the target changes. So a reactor's choices are found by working backwards from each arrival: to land a five point shot the moment they reach the third hex, start it five ticks before they get there. That makes the option list short and genuinely distinct, which is what an interface needs to show.

Settled

How much of this the player drives by hand: offering and resolving are separate calls. A window works out who could actually act — arc, reserve, affordable shot, mover in view at some tick — and hands each of them one option per way of firing plus a recommendation that maximises expected damage. Taking every recommendation is what the turn loop does today. Letting a player place their own, or an AI place better ones, is the same interface with something else calling it.

Settled, but watch it

An undetected mover crosses a watched arc untouched, because a watchman shoots at people rather than at bearings. The arc buys it a look at the moment of the crossing, not certainty about what is there.

This one is settled on reasoning rather than on play, so it is the first thing to re-examine once there is enough game to feel it. Two failure modes to watch for. If overwatch turns out to be too strong, the free look is the suspect — the bar to clear can go up, or the look can be taken away entirely, which reduces the trigger to a test of what the watchman already knew. If it turns out to be too weak, the gate is the suspect, and the fallback is a purely geometric trigger. Both halves are switches on the reaction model precisely so this can be tried rather than argued about, and neither has been tried yet.

10Built

Thrown

Beams, slugs, and what stops them

Science fiction in the register of Star Trek, Star Wars and Babylon 5 — people with technology rather than monsters. What the setting buys mechanically is permission for two weapon families to coexist that ought to have replaced one another, and to be held in tension.

BeamKinetic
ExamplesPhasers, blasters, blades at contact rangeSlugthrowers, railguns, autocannon

Force shields soak beam energy up to a capacity and recharge over a few turns, and do almost nothing about a solid object arriving fast. Ablative armour stops kinetic rounds and degrades as it does, and cooks under a sustained beam. Neither is a general answer, so a squad wants both kinds of weapon and the enemy composition decides which matters today.

More than two families

Beam against kinetic is the spine, but it is not the whole armoury, and the others are worth naming now because two of them need structure that does not exist yet.

WeaponWhat it is forWhat it needs building
Blades The silent kill. No report, no muzzle flash, nothing to relay. A powered blade is beam-family, so it goes through armour Adjacency and a stance check. Nothing new
Grenades Digging people out of the cover that makes them hard to shoot An arcing trace. You lob over a wall you cannot see through, so it is a different question from line of sight
Mines Denying ground while you are somewhere else. Shapes an approach without anybody watching it A reaction owned by the terrain rather than by a unit
Suppression Buying a crossing by making somebody keep their head down A per-unit state that taxes action points and accuracy

Grenades are the interesting one. Every trace so far has asked whether a straight line gets through, and an arc explicitly does not: the whole point of throwing one is to put it somewhere you cannot shoot. That wants a second query alongside the sight solver, answering whether a lobbed object clears the intervening walls and where it lands if it clips one.

Mines fold neatly into the reaction window in section 09 rather than needing a mechanism of their own. A mine is an overwatch that a unit is not standing behind: a trigger on a tile, resolved at the tick the mover enters it. Same clock, same resolution order.

Why this suits the systems already built

The two families give you away through different channels, and the detection model already has both. Kinetic fire is a loud noise event with a radius, heard through walls, reporting a place. Beam fire is a visual event: unmissable to anyone whose arc it crosses, and worth nothing at all to someone facing the other way.

So the quiet loadout is not a settled question. A suppressed slugthrower is silent until it fires, and then everyone within a hundred metres knows roughly where you are. A beam makes no sound and paints a line straight back to you for anyone looking. Which one belongs on a stealth mission depends on whether you expect to be heard or seen, and that is a question about the map and the enemy — the kind worth asking.

Destructible walls are already a flag on the wall profiles, so beams burning through cover needs no new structure. Neither does noise varying by surface. This is content and balance on top of what exists, not a rewrite.

Protection is per face, and the faces belong to the body

Both shields and armour are tracked per side rather than as one pool. Flanking then matters for damage as much as it does for detection, and it reuses the arc machinery that already decides what a unit notices — the same walk round the back that buys you an unnoticed approach also buys you the thin side of the armour.

The sides are named relative to the soldier, not to the compass: front, the two shoulders, the two flanks behind them, and the back. The plate that took a round from the north-east is the plate that happened to be pointing north-east at the time, and turning about puts it somewhere else. That is what makes a shield that has collapsed on one side worth turning away from the threat while it recharges, so turning is a combat decision and not just a looking one. Armour does not recover, so a side that has been chewed through stays chewed through, and a unit slowly runs out of good ones to present.

A body is a hexagon too

Which side a round finds is not a matter of rounding the bearing to the nearest of six. Stand in front of somebody and you are looking at their front plate square on and a sliver of each shoulder; stand toward a corner and you get two plates at equal, awkward angles. Weighting each face by how wide it looks from where the shot came from gives the shares exactly — a half and two quarters head-on, two halves corner-on — and the round is rolled against them when it is fired.

A kinetic round meeting a plate at an angle skips off it, losing up to half at grazing and a quarter off a shoulder. A beam does not: it lands where it lands and burns, and the angle of the surface is not its problem. So the geometry of an approach reads differently depending on what is being carried, which is one more reason a squad wants both families rather than settling on one.

The averages are deliberately flattened. Left raw, the arithmetic leaks: square on, half the rounds find an unangled plate and half skip off a shoulder, while on the corner every round arrives mildly angled — which works out slightly better for the shooter. That is an artefact of the abstraction and not something anyone should be playing around. A hexagon is bookkeeping for which plate wears, not a claim that soldiers are hexagonal, so every bearing is normalised against the head-on case and expects the same damage.

What still varies, and is the part worth having, is which side gets worn. Come at somebody square and you are chewing one plate with a bit of spill onto both shoulders; come at a corner and you split it evenly between two. Armour never recovers, so where the wear lands decides how quickly they run out of good sides — and a shield that has collapsed on one side can be turned away from the threat for a point, which is what makes turning a combat decision rather than a looking one.

Ordinary soldiers do not choose. You shoot at a man and the geometry decides where it lands, which is the right default and keeps the decision spatial rather than menu-driven. Placing a round on a named plate is something a soldier earns: it is off by default, and it costs accuracy to take the time to pick. You also cannot call a plate that is not in view, so a called shot at somebody's back still means getting behind them.

Open

How to show six facings of two defences without a spreadsheet on screen. Likely a ring around the unit, segmented, with shield as an outer band and armour as an inner one — readable at a glance, exact on hover. Six segments is probably too fine; three arcs of front, flanks and rear may carry the same decision at a fraction of the noise. The spread now has to be shown as well, since a shot is never at one plate: the sandbox lists all of them with their shares, which is honest and much too wordy.
