01Built

Godot, and why the engine barely matters

Turn-based tactics puts almost no load on a renderer or a physics solver, which is where Unity and Unreal earn their keep. Godot 4 is MIT-licensed, starts fast, iterates fast, and exports to all three desktop platforms from .NET. Desktop-only also erases its one real C# limitation, which is web export.

Unity does win on two things that matter for this game specifically: the asset store, and humanoid animation retargeting. Realistic soldiers with cover transitions and credible weapon handling are much cheaper to build there. That risk is real, and it arrives late — a year of systems work before it bites.

So the engine decision is deliberately made cheap to reverse. The simulation is a plain C# class library with zero engine references. Hex geometry, cover, movement, line of sight, detection, initiative, damage and AI all live in Hexcom.Core. Godot reads that state and draws it.

  • The rules are unit-testable headless — 85 tests run in 70 ms today.
  • Balance can be tuned by running thousands of AI-vs-AI matches with no window open.
  • If the art pipeline ever forces a move, only the view layer is lost.

This constraint is worth more than the engine choice, and it is what makes the engine choice safe. The rule to enforce forever: nothing under src/ may say using Godot;.

02Built

The grid, and the corner graph under it

Axial coordinates with cube maths, flat-top orientation. Flat-top is baked into the logical model because the corner-naming scheme below depends on orientation; the view can still render pointy-top by rotating the layout 30°, and the rules never notice.

The part that earns its complexity is corner identity. A hex has six corners, each shared by three hexes — so two corners per hex, and every corner in the plane is the due-east corner of exactly one hex or the due-west corner of exactly one hex. Naming them that way gives each corner a single canonical identity no matter which hex you ask through, which is what lets walls live on one global corner graph instead of being duplicated per tile.

Each corner connects to twelve others: three along hex sides, and nine interior chords, three in each of the three hexes it touches.

03Built

Cover is a chord, not a tile property

Because a hex's six corners each connect to the other five, a single hex can carry fifteen distinct wall segments. That is the entire cover vocabulary. A building face, a sandbag line clipping one corner, and a barricade running diagonally across a courtyard are all the same kind of object, differing only in which two corners they join.

Regions, and the transit rule

Chords cut a hex into regions, computed by walking the faces of the small planar graph the chords form. A region needs 60% of a hex before a unit can stand in it, which puts the natural cut sizes cleanly on either side of the line: 5/6 qualifies, 1/2 does not.

Sub-60% regions become transit nodes — pathed through, never valid as a destination. A wall bisecting a tile is therefore crossable and unoccupiable, exactly as intended, and the one-unit-per-tile invariant survives intact. No special case is written anywhere; it falls out of the area threshold.

Determining cover

Because walls are real segments in world space, cover is a geometry query rather than a lookup table: trace the line from shooter to target, collect the segments it crosses, take the most protective. A sandbag clipping one corner protects across a narrow arc; a building face protects across a wide one. This is strictly better than the usual six cover flags per tile, and it is the same code for every wall in the game.

04Built

Combat

Ten points a turn

Two actions in the XCOM style cannot price a ladder honestly against a footstep. Ten can. Firing costs about half a turn, so the archetypal turn is move a little, shoot, take cover — and the interesting decisions are all in what you give up to do the third thing.

Unspent points convert to reaction potential rather than evaporating. That single rule ties the economy to the stealth game: hanging back with points in hand is how you cover an approach, and sprinting is how you arrive somewhere with nothing left to answer with.

Firing costs are specified but not yet built: snap shot 3, standard 5, aimed 7, reload 3, change stance 1.

05Built

Movement is a graph of priced links

Not a grid of equal steps. Every connection between two places carries its own type and its own price, so a ladder eating most of a turn needs no special case anywhere — the pathfinder is plain Dijkstra over action points, and "how far can this unit get" and "how do I get there" are the same query.

Climbs, ledges and drops are generated automatically from floor heights. Ladders, stairs, doors and ziplines are authored. Terrain surcharges come from the ground type.

Rule learned the hard way

A wall never makes an otherwise impossible crossing possible, and where both a wall and a height change are in the way, the harder of the two sets the price. The first version let a soldier on the ground vault a rooftop parapet 3.5 m above their head. Caught by a test asserting the roof was ladder-only.

06Specified

Seeing first

The thing to design away from is the aggro pull — you open on two guards and find yourself fighting seven, because proximity to a trigger volume told a group of enemies about you instantly and for free. The fix is to make alertness travel as information rather than as a radius. Every enemy that knows about you learned it somewhere, through a channel the player can see and cut.

Awareness is a state, held per enemy

UnawarePatrol behaviour. No idea you exist.
SuspiciousSomething registered. Stops, scans, does not commit.
SearchingHolds a belief about where you were. Moves to check it.
AlertedKnows there is a threat. Takes cover, calls it in.
EngagedHas a live fix on a target and is shooting at it.

Detection accumulates

No binary spotted check. Each enemy builds detection against each of your units per turn, from distance, cover and concealment, your stance, how many points you spent moving, the noise you made, light level, their perception, and whether you are inside their facing cone. Crossing a threshold advances the state one rung; silence lets it decay.

Noise is its own channel with its own propagation — ground type has a noise factor already, gravel is loud and grass is quiet — and noise produces an investigate goal at a location, not a global alert.

Alerts propagate through channels you can cut

  • Line of sight — an enemy who sees an alerted comrade takes on some of what they know.
  • Voice — short range, blocked by walls.
  • Radio — map-wide, but it belongs to specific people. Killing the radio operator quietly is a tactic, and target prioritisation becomes an information decision rather than a damage one.

The interface is half the mechanic

None of this reads as a stealth game unless the player can see it. Enemy vision cones as currently estimated; a detection meter per one of your units, filling; last-known-position ghosts showing what each enemy believes rather than what is true. The gap between the ghost and the truth is the thing the player is actually playing.

Attacking from concealment gives a damage and accuracy bonus, plus the initiative payoff in the next section.

07Specified

Open

Initiative, and the ambush it breaks

Individual interleaved initiative in the D&D sense: one of yours, two of theirs, one of yours, and so on, ordered by a stat plus a roll, modified by gear weight. Rolled at the start of a fight, re-rolled or drifting each round so the order does not calcify.

The tension worth naming

Interleaved initiative and stealth pull against each other. XCOM ambushes feel good precisely because concealment breaks and all six of your units fire before anyone reacts. Interleaving destroys that: the enemy acts between your shots, and a coordinated opening becomes impossible to execute.

The fix is an explicit Ambush action. A unit spends points to hold a shot against an arc. When any ambushing unit fires — by choice, or because they were spotted — every other unit holding on that target resolves in the same window, before the enemy acts. Alpha strikes survive; the turn structure does not need a special case; and the AP economy already produces the resource it spends, because held points were going to become reaction potential anyway.

Undetected units also take a large initiative bonus in the first round after contact, which is the mechanical payoff for having played the approach well.

Open  Build the turn order as a priority queue over next-act time, even though round-based initiative is all that ships first. It costs nothing now and leaves the door open to a continuous time-unit system — the Battle Brothers or Final Fantasy Tactics model, where a cheap action comes back around sooner — without rewriting the loop. Whether that continuous model is better here is genuinely unresolved; it is deeper, and it is harder to read at a glance.
