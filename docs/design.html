01Built

Godot, and why the engine barely matters

Turn-based tactics puts almost no load on a renderer or a physics solver, which is where Unity and Unreal earn their keep. Godot 4 is MIT-licensed, starts fast, iterates fast, and exports to all three desktop platforms from .NET. Desktop-only also erases its one real C# limitation, which is web export.

Unity does win on two things that matter for this game specifically: the asset store, and humanoid animation retargeting. Realistic soldiers with cover transitions and credible weapon handling are much cheaper to build there. That risk is real, and it arrives late — a year of systems work before it bites.

So the engine decision is deliberately made cheap to reverse. The simulation is a plain C# class library with zero engine references. Hex geometry, cover, movement, line of sight, detection, initiative, damage and AI all live in Hexcom.Core. Godot reads that state and draws it.

  • The rules are unit-testable headless — 381 of them, run in about ten seconds today, most of it the AI. The maps, the missions and the matches fought on them bring 42 more, and a batch of matches that is off by default and takes an hour.
  • Balance can be tuned by running thousands of AI-vs-AI matches with no window open.
  • If the art pipeline ever forces a move, only the view layer is lost.

This constraint is worth more than the engine choice, and it is what makes the engine choice safe. The rule to enforce forever: nothing under src/ may say using Godot;.

02Built

The grid, and the corner graph under it

Axial coordinates with cube maths, flat-top orientation. Flat-top is baked into the logical model because the corner-naming scheme below depends on orientation; the view can still render pointy-top by rotating the layout 30°, and the rules never notice.

The part that earns its complexity is corner identity. A hex has six corners, each shared by three hexes — so two corners per hex, and every corner in the plane is the due-east corner of exactly one hex or the due-west corner of exactly one hex. Naming them that way gives each corner a single canonical identity no matter which hex you ask through, which is what lets walls live on one global corner graph instead of being duplicated per tile.

Each corner connects to twelve others: three along hex sides, and nine interior chords, three in each of the three hexes it touches.

A hex is one metre of radius — two metres corner to corner, 1.73 m between centres. One horizontal world unit is one metre, necessarily rather than by choice: the sight solver builds a vector from a layout position and a floor height in metres and takes distances across the result, so the layout’s units are metres. Rendering scale is a separate number that must never reach the rules. The figure itself is the one the tests had always used, chosen at last rather than inherited: it puts a soldier and their kit in a tile without crowding, and it makes a stride across a hex a stride.

03Built

Cover is a chord, not a tile property

Because a hex's six corners each connect to the other five, a single hex can carry fifteen distinct wall segments. That is the entire cover vocabulary. A building face, a sandbag line clipping one corner, and a barricade running diagonally across a courtyard are all the same kind of object, differing only in which two corners they join.

Regions, and the transit rule

Chords cut a hex into regions, computed by walking the faces of the small planar graph the chords form. A region needs 60% of a hex before a unit can stand in it, which puts the natural cut sizes cleanly on either side of the line: 5/6 qualifies, 1/2 does not.

Sub-60% regions become transit nodes — pathed through, never valid as a destination. A wall bisecting a tile is therefore crossable and unoccupiable, exactly as intended, and the one-unit-per-tile invariant survives intact. No special case is written anywhere; it falls out of the area threshold.

Determining cover

Because walls are real segments in world space, cover is a geometry query rather than a lookup table: trace the line from shooter to target, collect the segments it crosses, take the most protective. A sandbag clipping one corner protects across a narrow arc; a building face protects across a wide one. This is strictly better than the usual six cover flags per tile, and it is the same code for every wall in the game.

04Built

Reload

Fifty points a turn

Two actions in the XCOM style cannot price a ladder honestly against a footstep. A stride costs five and a turn is fifty, so ten hexes of open ground is a whole turn spent doing nothing else. Firing costs about half a turn, so the archetypal turn is move a little, shoot, take cover — and the interesting decisions are all in what you give up to do the third thing.

It started at ten, with a stride at one, and that turned out to be the wrong number for a reason worth recording: the cheapest action sets the resolution of everything else. Nothing could be priced below a stride, so turning on the spot cost a full step; and no per-soldier multiplier could make anybody quicker over open ground, only slower, because one point cannot be reduced. At five a turn is worth two, going flat is worth three strides a hex, and a multiplier anywhere from three fifths to double lands on a distinct number.

It matters twice over, because action points are also the clock a reaction window runs on. A ten hex run is fifty ticks long rather than ten, so placing a shot to land just as somebody clears a wall is a real choice rather than a rounding accident. Every ratio survived the change untouched — the whole price list was multiplied by five — so this cost nothing but the arithmetic in the tests.

Unspent points convert to reaction potential rather than evaporating. That single rule ties the economy to the stealth game: hanging back with points in hand is how you cover an approach, and sprinting is how you arrive somewhere with nothing left to answer with.

Firing costs: snap shot 15, standard 25, aimed 35. Change stance 2, turn on the spot 2, declare an overwatch arc 5. Reloading is specified but not built. Seven tenths of whatever is left over becomes reaction potential — see section 09 for why that fraction.

05Built

Movement is a graph of priced links

Not a grid of equal steps. Every connection between two places carries its own type and its own price, so a ladder eating most of a turn needs no special case anywhere — the pathfinder is plain Dijkstra over action points, and "how far can this unit get" and "how do I get there" are the same query.

Climbs, ledges and drops are generated automatically from floor heights. Ladders, stairs, doors and ziplines are authored. Terrain surcharges come from the ground type.

Rule learned the hard way

A wall never makes an otherwise impossible crossing possible, and where both a wall and a height change are in the way, the harder of the two sets the price. The first version let a soldier on the ground vault a rooftop parapet 3.5 m above their head. Caught by a test asserting the roof was ladder-only.

06Built

One trace, two answers

Can I see you, and how exposed are you. Those are the same question asked at two heights, so they come out of one trace. The sight line is flattened onto the ground and intersected with the wall segments it crosses. At each crossing the top of the wall is projected back along the line onto the target as a waterline: everything below it is hidden, everything above it can be seen and shot.

Cover is graded by how much of the silhouette falls below that line. The target is invisible when an opaque wall submerges all of it. That one construction replaces the usual pile of special cases.

What comes free

None of the following is written down anywhere in the rules. Each is what the geometry already says, which is the test that the model is the right one.

  • Stance. A standing soldier is visible over waist-high cover. The same soldier prone behind it is gone.
  • Range. A low wall far down the line hides nothing, because the waterline it projects is below the target's feet by then.
  • Elevation. High ground cancels low cover on its own, and leaves head-high cover mostly intact.
  • Point blank. An attacker in the next hex shoots down over the wall their target is hugging, so cover drops to almost nothing.
  • Storeys. A wall projects a band, not just a line, so a raised walkway can be seen underneath.

Materials describe matter, not situations

A wall profile says whether light passes through it and the best grade it can ever give. It does not say whether you can see over it — that is the trace's job. So sandbags are opaque and capped at half cover; chain link is transparent and capped at light, however much of you is behind it; smoke is opaque, capped at light, and you can walk straight through it.

Bug found by a test

A silhouette exactly as tall as the wall hiding it produces a hidden share of 0.9999999999999999, which read as a sliver of head showing over the top. A soldier was visible through a solid building wall. Fixed with an epsilon, and the boundary now has a test of its own.

What it hands the interface

Every trace returns the wall responsible for the grade, so the interface can point at the actual sandbag instead of showing an abstract shield, and an exposure figure from zero to one. That figure turned out to be the load-bearing one: it drives hit chance directly, it is what the detection model multiplies a look by, and it is why stance and cover need no separate rules in either. Both answers are symmetric and side-agnostic, because detection runs the same query in the enemy's direction — and the AI must be restricted to exactly what the interface can show you.

A trace is pure geometry over the map and the layout, so the solver remembers every pair of vantages it has answered until the map changes. That is what pays for the scorer asking about every step of every route: the routes share their hexes, and a briefed waystation match that took two hundred seconds tracing the same lines takes about ten.

07Built

Seeing first

The thing to design away from is the aggro pull — you open on two guards and find yourself fighting seven, because proximity to a trigger volume told a group of enemies about you instantly and for free. The fix is to make alertness travel as information rather than as a radius. Every enemy that knows about you learned it somewhere, through a channel the player can see and cut.

Awareness is a state, held per enemy

UnawarePatrol behaviour. No idea you exist.
SuspiciousSomething registered. Stops, scans, does not commit.
SearchingHolds a belief about where you were. Moves to check it.

EngagedHas a live fix on a target and is shooting at it.

Detection accumulates

No binary spotted check. Each enemy builds detection against each of your units per turn, from distance, cover and concealment, your stance, how many points you spent moving, the noise you made, light level, their perception, and whether you are inside their facing cone. Crossing a threshold advances the state one rung; silence lets it decay.

Noise is its own channel with its own propagation — ground type has a noise factor already, gravel is loud and grass is quiet — and noise produces an investigate goal at a location, not a global alert.

Alerts propagate through channels you can cut

  • Line of sight — an enemy who sees an alerted comrade takes on some of what they know.
  • Voice — short range, blocked by walls.
  • Radio — map-wide, but it belongs to specific people. Killing the radio operator quietly is a tactic, and target prioritisation becomes an information decision rather than a damage one.

The window

Looking happens on the observer's own turn, not continuously. A sentry that has already acted this round will not notice you until it comes round again — so the turn order strip is not just a readout, it is a tool. Reading it to find the gap and crossing the open ground inside it is meant to be a real decision.

Hearing does not wait. Move loudly and everyone in earshot knows something happened immediately, which is what stops the window from being a free pass.

Asymmetric information, deliberately

The player reads their own soldiers' exposure as an exact figure, because that is information about themselves and hiding it would be fog rather than tension. What an enemy believes comes back coarse: a rung on the ladder above, a marker for where they last had contact, and how many rounds stale it is. An exact certainty number would turn the approach into arithmetic.

That split is enforced in the code rather than left to discipline. The full record, with the certainty figure, is what the rules read, and what the AI reads about its own soldier’s contacts. What the AI reads about the enemy’s contacts on its soldier is the same coarse readout the interface goes through, with no number on it — it read the number for two increments, the interface audit caught it, and section 11 says what it cost.

The last-known-position marker is the payoff: it is where an enemy thinks you are, and it goes stale the moment you move. The gap between the marker and the truth is the thing the stealth game is actually played in.

There is a third case, and the rule above did not name it. Your own exposure is exact and the enemy’s alarm is a rung — but what your soldier holds on an enemy is neither, and the interface would like to quote it: how much is still left to learn about this contact. It is read exactly. The principle underneath the split is not what the number is about, it is whose knowledge it is: your side’s knowledge is yours, in both directions, and blurring what your own soldier has worked out would be fog about yourself. The other side’s knowledge, in both directions, stays coarse. So the contract reads: you get your own side’s certainty as a figure and theirs as a rung, and the marker they hold on you is the one thing of theirs you see at all.

Facing, so a position can be flanked

A unit looks one way. Inside a hundred and twenty degrees it notices things at full rate; out to two hundred it catches them in the corner of an eye; behind, at a twelfth. Coming at a sentry from the rear is worth an order of magnitude, which is what makes the long way round a real option rather than flavour.

Facing decides how readily something is noticed, never whether it could be seen at all. Line of sight stays pure geometry in the sight solver; who is paying attention to what is a question about people, and it lives in the awareness model. Moving turns you to face your line of travel for nothing; watching one way while standing still costs a point, and that is the whole decision.

Carrying yourself low is priced, not free. A crouch costs three fifths again on every traversal and going flat costs three times, so a crawler covers three hexes where an upright soldier covers ten. That is the whole trade the stealth game runs on: prone you are harder to see, harder to notice, quieter and lower than most cover, and the bill is the ground you do not cross. Until it was charged for there was no reason ever to stand back up.

The trap, and it was live for a while, is pricing noise off what a move cost. Crawling is three times the price, so a crawler spending three times the points to cross one hex came out very nearly as loud as somebody strolling over it — the stance cancelling itself out and the reason to go flat with it. Effort is a property of the ground crossed and how it was crossed, not of the bill; a slow soldier is not a noisy one.

Still to come

Attacking from concealment giving a damage and accuracy bonus, on top of the initiative payoff in the next section. Noise currently ignores walls entirely — you hear people through a building, which is right, but a closed door should count for something.

08Built

Initiative, and the ambush it breaks

Individual interleaved initiative in the D&D sense: one of yours, two of theirs, one of yours, and so on, ordered by a stat plus a roll, modified by gear weight. Rerolled every round so the order does not calcify.

What ships is a queue over a battle clock rather than a list of sides taking it in turns. A round is a thousand ticks wide; initiative is a rating plus a ten-sided roll less encumbrance, and a higher result buys a place earlier in the round. Rebooking happens the moment a unit becomes active rather than when its turn ends, so the queue always holds one future turn per unit and the order strip can look ahead — which the player needs, because deciding whether to move now or hold depends on who acts next.

Every roll comes from one seeded generator owned by the battle. A whole fight replays identically from a seed and a list of commands, which is the property the headless balance runs depend on.

The tension worth naming

Interleaved initiative and stealth pull against each other. XCOM ambushes feel good precisely because concealment breaks and all six of your units fire before anyone reacts. Interleaving destroys that: the enemy acts between your shots, and a coordinated opening becomes impossible to execute.

The fix is an explicit Ambush action. A unit spends points to hold a shot against an arc. When any ambushing unit fires — by choice, or because they were spotted — every other unit holding on that target resolves in the same window, before the enemy acts. Alpha strikes survive; the turn structure does not need a special case; and the AP economy already produces the resource it spends, because held points were going to become reaction potential anyway.

Undetected units also take a large initiative bonus in the first round after contact, which is the mechanical payoff for having played the approach well.

Open  The clock is in place, so moving to a continuous time-unit system — the Battle Brothers or Final Fantasy Tactics model, where a cheap action brings you round sooner — means replacing the round arithmetic in one method with actAt += cost. Nothing else changes. Whether that model is better here is still unresolved: it is deeper, and it is harder to read at a glance. The decision can wait until there is enough game to feel the difference.

09Built

Three ways to act out of turn

Reactions all draw on the same resource: action points left over at the end of your turn. That closes the loop on the economy — sprinting somewhere leaves you with nothing to answer with, and hanging back with points in hand is how you cover an approach. The choice to reserve is made before you know whether it will pay, which is the right shape for a decision.

How much of the leftover actually carries is a difficulty setting, and it applies to both sides. Turned up, the whole battlefield is twitchier: every mistake gets answered and committing to a move becomes frightening. Turned down, turns feel more like turns. It changes the texture of the game rather than handing the player a handicap.

The dial settled at seven tenths. It is a share rather than a fixed number, which matters more than the number does: the allowance is expected to vary by soldier once stats, training and kit feed into it, so nothing here may assume the standard fifty. What is calibrated is the shape — seven tenths puts the three rungs of the movement economy on either side of the fire mode prices, so each is a real decision rather than a rounding accident. Against a fifty point turn it lands exactly: hold everything and thirty-five bank, which is an aimed shot; spend half the turn moving and seventeen bank, which covers a snap shot with change; sprint and nothing banks. A floor cuts anything under a couple of strides, because a leftover nothing can be spent on is noise and offering it is worse than rounding it away.

The price list is not the same thing as what a given soldier pays for it. A ladder is a ladder and a snap shot is a snap shot — that is a fact about the world — but what this trooper spends climbing one or getting one off is about them, their training and their kit, and it lives on the unit as a pair of multipliers. So a scout who is quick over ground and slow to bring a weapon to bear, and a gunner who is the other way round, spend the same fifty points on entirely different turns. A stabilised mount that takes a standard shot from twenty-five points to twenty is a multiplier on the wearer, not a change to what a standard shot is.

Pricing is applied when a route is costed rather than baked into the movement graph. The graph describes the ground and is built once for the map; rebuilding it per soldier to say that one of them is slow would be absurd. And because cost is time inside a reaction window, none of this is only economy: a watchman slow on the trigger places its shot later on the mover's timeline and catches them further along the route. Being the slow one has a consequence measured in ticks.

Open

Prices are absolute while the allowance is not. A soldier given more points does not merely move further — they bank a larger reserve, afford better shots out of it, and place them earlier on a mover's timeline, so the advantage compounds across three systems at once. Whether that is the right reward for being quick, or wants a counterweight, is a question for whenever allowances start to differ in earnest. The resolution half of this went away with the rescale: at a stride of five a multiplier from three fifths to double lands on a distinct number in both directions.

Keeping it from becoming a cascade

The failure mode is obvious enough to design against up front: every action triggering a volley of responses, each of which is itself an action, and a turn taking five minutes to resolve. Four things hold it back, and only the last two are rules.

  • The economy does most of the work. Reactions are meant to be a feature of the approach, not of the firefight. Move slowly and methodically and the bank is real; once the shooting starts almost everything gets spent, and what is left is usually below the price of even a snap shot.
  • A reserve is spent, not refreshed. Points used reacting are gone until that unit's next turn, so a unit reacts about once a round however many chances it gets. Nobody answers the same move twice.
  • Only units that could actually act are asked. Reserve in hand, the mover crossing the arc they care about at some point in the window, and something they can afford — that is a short list most of the time, and usually an empty one mid-firefight.
  • Being startled is a crossing, not a state. The involuntary one is the real risk, because nobody has to set it up and everybody has it. It fires on the single moment a contact goes from below the bar to above it — so it happens at most once per opponent, and never at all once the shooting has started and everybody already knows where everybody is.

In practice the third one does most of the visible work. Building the offer list for a window means asking every hostile whether it holds an arc, has anything banked, can afford a shot, and would have the mover in view at some tick — and the answer is usually no for everyone, so most moves open a window that resolves to nothing at all.

Ambush

The answer to the problem in section 08. A squad arms against an agreed trigger and waits; when one of them fires, every armed unit resolves in that same window before the enemy acts. Alpha strikes survive interleaved initiative, and the turn structure needs no special case — the reserved points were already going to become reaction potential.

Arming costs points up front on top of the shot held back, so setting an ambush is a turn spent not advancing — for every member of it. Spring it early and the coordination is wasted; wait too long and somebody sees you first.

Structurally it is the one reaction that needed something new, and it turned out to need less than expected. A window is built on a committed move, and an ambush has no mover: the target is standing still. So it gets a route of no length — one instant, the same timeline — and everything else follows. Placements still land in the order of what they cost, which means the snap shots go before the aimed ones, and if the cheap ones put the target down the expensive ones are never taken and those reserves are never spent.

There are two ways to be the one who says now. The member taking a turn can choose the moment deliberately, and pays out of that turn while everybody else pays out of the reserves they banked when they armed. Or somebody simply walks into an armed arc, in which case nobody is having a turn and the whole squad answers out of reserve. Both open the same window; the difference is only which pocket the first shot comes from.

Springing calls the contact in before anybody fires, which is what makes the trap a squad action rather than a coincidence. An ambusher has to be told — by radio, by shout, or by having eyes on the target already — or it does not fire, so the radio operator is back at the centre of things and an ambusher posted somewhere nobody can reach is decoration. And because everybody but the springer pays out of a reserve, an ambusher who got bored and spent its turn going somewhere is still armed, still looking the right way, and out of the ambush.

Surprise

The only involuntary one, and the only one nobody has to plan for. When a unit suddenly registers an opponent it may spend part of what it has left, immediately: turn to face, fire, drop into cover, or shout.

Less of the reserve is available than for a deliberate reaction, because reacting is not planning — half of it, and no aiming bonus. The point is that ambushing a competent soldier should not be free. Walking round the back of a sentry deserves a reward; catching one with nothing at all in hand ought to take more than proximity.

The trigger is a crossing, and getting that right is most of what makes surprise work rather than flood the game. Not "can see them" — a soldier already being tracked is not a surprise however plainly they walk past. Not "learned something" either, or every step of an opponent climbing your awareness ladder would startle you again. It is the single moment a contact goes from below the bar to above it: once per opponent, and never once the shooting has started and everybody already knows.

Two bars, not one, because what you may do depends on how sure you are. Something registering at the edge of what you can make out is enough to duck or spin round; it is nowhere near enough to shoot at, and a soldier who fires at every half-seen shape is not a competent one. So a crawler at forty metres will make a sentry twitch and will not draw fire for it — which is exactly the outcome the careful approach is played for.

It reaches wider than an overwatch does, out to the whole arc a unit can see into at all rather than the slice it is properly attending to. Catching something in the corner of an eye is the case being modelled, and it is the only one where turning to face it is worth an action. Behind is still behind: walk round the back and nothing flinches.

The beat before a surprise starts is where "the weapon was already pointed" becomes mechanical rather than a claim. Without it, a sentry with nothing declared answers exactly as fast as one that spent a point and its whole turn watching the right ground, and overwatch is left buying only the bonus. With it, the same soldier firing the same snap shot lands later and catches the runner further along a route it is already committed to.

There is one more thing the reserve turns out to be doing, and it was not designed in so much as noticed afterwards. A reactor gets its look at the crossing only if it has points banked — so the reserve is alertness as well as reaction potential. A soldier who spent the lot is head down and busy and does not clock you going past. Hanging back keeps your eyes up as well as your options open, which is the same decision the whole economy is built around.

Overwatch

A watchman declares an arc and holds it. Anything moving inside is shot at, at a bonus, because the weapon was already pointed there. A narrower arc aims better, so the decision is how much ground to try to cover against how well.

This is where facing earns its keep twice: the arc a unit is watching is the same arc it notices things in, so an overwatch position is also a detection position — and flanking it defeats both at once.

ArcWidthTo hitWhat it is
Narrow60°×1.35One approach, watched properly

The bonus multiplies rather than adds, so overwatching pays best on a shot that was plausible anyway and never turns a hopeless one into a good one. Declaring costs a single point out of the turn, plus the usual price of turning if the arc is not where the unit was already looking. That one point matters more than it looks: it means a watchman banks six, so an overwatch shot is never an aimed one. Overwatch pays in the aim bonus instead of in aiming time, and a narrow arc is worth roughly what taking your time is worth — at snap shot speed. That is the whole reason it is strong.

A watchman only fires at somebody it has actually noticed. Geometry alone is not enough: without that gate, a sentry blazes away at a prone crawler forty metres out with four per cent of itself showing — someone the detection model says it has not registered at all — and overwatch becomes the one place in the game where something happens to a hidden soldier without the awareness ladder being consulted.

The gate needs a companion to be worth anything, though, and it is worth naming why. Looking normally happens only on your own turn, so a soldier who was behind a building when the sentry last looked could step out into a declared arc and cross it untouched — the canonical overwatch situation, defeated by the turn order. So holding an arc is looking down it: movement inside gets a proper detection check at the tick it happens, through the ordinary model. Stance, cover, range, exposure and perception all still apply, so a careful approach is as invisible to a watchman as to anybody else, and a jog across the open is not. That extra look outside your own turn is precisely what the point and the held reserve bought.

Arc edges are exclusive, and decided rather than left to arithmetic. Hex bearings are exact multiples of sixty degrees, so a place sitting precisely on the edge of a hundred and twenty degree arc is the common case and not a rare one — the maths that gets there lands on 60.00000000000001 as readily as on 60. The edge belongs to the wider arc, in declared overwatches and in attention cones alike. A standard arc held toward one spoke therefore does not reach the next spoke over: cover that ground and you declare a wider arc, and shoot worse down all of it.

Action points are time, inside the window

A reaction does not resolve before or after the move that triggered it. It resolves during it, on a shared clock, and the currency of that clock is action points.

The move that triggered the window is already committed, so both sides know the future for its duration. A five point run is five ticks long, and the route is known: at tick three the mover is three hexes along, wherever that is. A reactor spending from its reserve places its actions on that same timeline. Whatever it starts at tick t and costs k resolves at t plus k — against wherever the mover will be by then, not where it is now.

The standard shot is the better shot and it misses the moment: it goes off at tick twenty-five, by which time the target has arrived and is behind cover. The snap shot is worse in every respect except the one that matters here — it lands at tick fifteen, while the target is still two hexes short of the wall, and leaves ten points to duck away afterwards. This is what a watchman actually chooses, unprompted: the recommendation scores each shot against where the runner will be when it lands, and picks the cheap one the moment the destination has cover. See section 11 for what “scores” means.

This is what makes cheap actions worth having. A snap shot that is simply a worse aimed shot is never chosen; a snap shot that is the only one that connects is a real decision. The same arithmetic explains why overwatch is strong: the weapon is already pointed, so the shot is cheap, so it lands early in the window while the target is still in the open.

It gives the mover something to think about too. A long run through open ground hands every reactor a wide window; a two point shuffle hands them almost nothing but barely gets anywhere. How far to commit becomes the question, rather than simply where to end up.

Structurally this costs less than it sounds. A route already arrives as a list of links with an action point price on each, so "where is the mover at cumulative cost three" is arithmetic on data that exists. What is new is a window object that holds the committed route, collects each reactor's placements, and resolves them in tick order.

It turned out cheaper still than that, because of one decision worth recording: resolving a window actually walks the mover along its route, stopping at each tick something lands and firing from there. The mover is genuinely standing where the timeline says it is, so line of sight, cover grade, range band and which plate the round arrives at all come back from the same code that answers them on anybody's turn. There is no hypothetical-position path through the shooting rules and no second version of the geometry to keep honest. A runner dropped three ticks into a four tick run simply stops there, one hex short of the wall it was running for.

Only the ticks at which the mover arrives somewhere are worth considering — in between, nothing about the target changes. So a reactor's choices are found by working backwards from each arrival: to land a five point shot the moment they reach the third hex, start it five ticks before they get there. That makes the option list short and genuinely distinct, which is what an interface needs to show.

A window a person can answer

Offering and resolving are separate calls, and the gap between them is now reachable. A move is committed — the route priced, the points spent, the offers made, nothing placed — and stays that way until somebody resolves it. Moving is those two with every recommendation taken in between, which is what a headless match and the turn loop want; an interface calls them separately and does its own placing in the gap.

It has to be a state the battle sits in rather than a question asked inside a call, and that is forced rather than preferred. Input in a game engine arrives across frames: a click is a later event, not a return value, so a callback invoked from inside the move would have to stop the whole engine until the player chose. A committed move is something the interface can draw for as many frames as it likes.

The seam reaches through the AI as well, because the case a player cares about most is the enemy’s move: a hostile walks across your sentry’s arc and your sentry is the one being offered the shot. A commander that resolved its own windows would run straight past the seam, so it can be told to hand them out instead — the turn stops with the window open and resumes once it has been answered. And what it hands back is what each order did as well as what it was chosen on, so the enemy’s turn can be narrated rather than inferred from a vitality label changing.

Holding fire is now an option like any other, offered wherever there was already a choice. It settles a question that had been open since reactions were built: nothing could decline one, so a reactor took its best option even when every option scored below zero — which happens, because a beam that will be soaked entirely is worth about what it costs. An explicit action rather than an empty recommendation, because it is a thing a player picks on purpose and the scorer prices at exactly nothing, so it wins by arithmetic whenever every answer is worse than nothing.

It is offered to the deliberate reactions only. An overwatch and an ambush are held shots and may be kept. Surprise is the involuntary one, and the whole of what it models is that a soldier caught out does something rather than nothing — offering it the chance to do nothing is offering it the chance not to flinch, and measured, it takes it: a dive costs two points and buys a discounted share of one shot, which on open ground comes out just under nothing.

Settled, but watch it

An undetected mover crosses a watched arc untouched, because a watchman shoots at people rather than at bearings. The arc buys it a look at the moment of the crossing, not certainty about what is there.

This one is settled on reasoning rather than on play, so it is the first thing to re-examine once there is enough game to feel it. Two failure modes to watch for. If overwatch turns out to be too strong, the free look is the suspect — the bar to clear can go up, or the look can be taken away entirely, which reduces the trigger to a test of what the watchman already knew. If it turns out to be too weak, the gate is the suspect, and the fallback is a purely geometric trigger. Both halves are switches on the reaction model precisely so this can be tried rather than argued about, and neither has been tried yet.

Suppression

Beams, slugs, and what stops them

Science fiction in the register of Star Trek, Star Wars and Babylon 5 — people with technology rather than monsters. What the setting buys mechanically is permission for two weapon families to coexist that ought to have replaced one another, and to be held in tension.

Force shields soak beam energy up to a capacity and recharge over a few turns, and do almost nothing about a solid object arriving fast. Ablative armour stops kinetic rounds and degrades as it does, and cooks under a sustained beam. Neither is a general answer, so a squad wants both kinds of weapon and the enemy composition decides which matters today.

More than two families

Beam against kinetic is the spine, but it is not the whole armoury, and the others are worth naming now because two of them need structure that does not exist yet.

Grenades were the interesting one, and the second query is built. Every other trace asks whether a straight line gets through and an arc explicitly does not: the whole point of throwing one is to put it somewhere you cannot shoot. So the lob solver crosses exactly the walls the sight solver crosses and asks a different question of each — whether the top is above the parabola rather than above the line. The free parameter is the apex above the straight line from hand to landing point, and a soldier throws as flat as the obstacles allow, so the answer is the lowest apex that clears everything. On open ground that is nought and the arc degenerates back into the sight line.

Two consequences worth having are arithmetic rather than rules. The closer you stand to the wall, the higher you must throw, because the divisor collapses toward the ends of the throw: hugging a three metre wall and throwing seventeen metres wants seven and a half metres of arc and fails, where the same wall halfway along wants about two. And a throw that cannot clear clips and drops short, on the near side of the wall it caught, which is how a grenade ends up at your own feet.

Where it lands, the wave reaches each soldier by three things the game already had: distance from the burst, what stands between, and how much of them is standing up in it. The burst traces outward through the same waterline arithmetic, so a wall shelters you from a charge on its far side and does nothing about one lobbed over; and going flat quarters what you catch, because a prone silhouette is a quarter the height of a standing one. Neither needed a rule about explosions and cover. A blast then hits the faces a round hits and goes through the layers a round goes through, so fragmentation is kinetic and is the answer to a shielded soldier exactly as a beam is the answer to an armoured one.

A charge is the first thing in this game that runs out, which turns out to be the load-bearing part. A scorer that priced only the action points threw both grenades at the first soldier it saw; what it was missing was that still having one is worth something. Priced at about one clean rifle shot, the distinction the design wants falls out: against a man in the open the rifle is nearly as good and free, and against a man behind a wall the rifle is worth nothing at all. A grenade is for the shot you cannot take.

Mines folded into the reaction window in section 09 rather than needing a mechanism of their own, exactly as forecast. A mine is an overwatch that a unit is not standing behind: a trigger on a tile, resolved at the tick the mover enters it, on the same clock and in the same resolution order — the terrain going first where a mine and a held shot land together, because the mine is the mover’s own foot arriving and everybody else had to decide. What it needed was not a mechanism but an owner, since everything else in a window is offered to a unit out of that unit’s reserve.

And an explosion is heard from where it went off rather than from who set it off, which is the one way in this game to make a great deal of noise somewhere you are not standing. Everybody in earshot marks the thrower at the crater, which is wrong and is meant to be. Being blown up does not tell you who did it, unlike being shot at, which settles the question.

Why this suits the systems already built

The two families give you away through different channels, and the detection model already has both. Kinetic fire is a loud noise event with a radius, heard through walls, reporting a place. Beam fire is a visual event: unmissable to anyone whose arc it crosses, and worth nothing at all to someone facing the other way.

So the quiet loadout is not a settled question. A suppressed slugthrower is silent until it fires, and then everyone within earshot knows roughly where you are. A beam makes no sound and paints a line straight back to you for anyone looking. Which one belongs on a stealth mission depends on whether you expect to be heard or seen, and that is a question about the map and the enemy — the kind worth asking.

Open  How far earshot actually is. This paragraph said a hundred metres for a long time and the code says eighteen: a slug rifle at 45 loudness against 0.4 metres per point. Measured on an 85 m map, that makes a firefight at one gate inaudible at a barn 24 m away — so the one cost the weapons table gives a rifle over a carbine, that it is heard, is nearly free. A hundred makes every shot on that map heard by everybody. Somewhere between is where a shot at the gate wakes the barn and not the tower, and the number here now matches the code rather than the intention while that is decided.

Destructible walls are already a flag on the wall profiles, so beams burning through cover needs no new structure. Neither does noise varying by surface. This is content and balance on top of what exists, not a rewrite.

Protection is per face, and the faces belong to the body

Both shields and armour are tracked per side rather than as one pool. Flanking then matters for damage as much as it does for detection, and it reuses the arc machinery that already decides what a unit notices — the same walk round the back that buys you an unnoticed approach also buys you the thin side of the armour.

The sides are named relative to the soldier, not to the compass: front, the two shoulders, the two flanks behind them, and the back. The plate that took a round from the north-east is the plate that happened to be pointing north-east at the time, and turning about puts it somewhere else. That is what makes a shield that has collapsed on one side worth turning away from the threat while it recharges, so turning is a combat decision and not just a looking one. Armour does not recover, so a side that has been chewed through stays chewed through, and a unit slowly runs out of good ones to present.

A body is a hexagon too

Which side a round finds is not a matter of rounding the bearing to the nearest of six. Stand in front of somebody and you are looking at their front plate square on and a sliver of each shoulder; stand toward a corner and you get two plates at equal, awkward angles. Weighting each face by how wide it looks from where the shot came from gives the shares exactly — a half and two quarters head-on, two halves corner-on — and the round is rolled against them when it is fired.

A kinetic round meeting a plate at an angle skips off it, losing up to half at grazing and a quarter off a shoulder. A beam does not: it lands where it lands and burns, and the angle of the surface is not its problem. So the geometry of an approach reads differently depending on what is being carried, which is one more reason a squad wants both families rather than settling on one.

The averages are deliberately flattened. Left raw, the arithmetic leaks: square on, half the rounds find an unangled plate and half skip off a shoulder, while on the corner every round arrives mildly angled — which works out slightly better for the shooter. That is an artefact of the abstraction and not something anyone should be playing around. A hexagon is bookkeeping for which plate wears, not a claim that soldiers are hexagonal, so every bearing is normalised against the head-on case and expects the same damage.

What still varies, and is the part worth having, is which side gets worn. Come at somebody square and you are chewing one plate with a bit of spill onto both shoulders; come at a corner and you split it evenly between two. Armour never recovers, so where the wear lands decides how quickly they run out of good sides — and a shield that has collapsed on one side can be turned away from the threat for a point, which is what makes turning a combat decision rather than a looking one.

Ordinary soldiers do not choose. You shoot at a man and the geometry decides where it lands, which is the right default and keeps the decision spatial rather than menu-driven. Placing a round on a named plate is something a soldier earns: it is off by default, and it costs accuracy to take the time to pick. You also cannot call a plate that is not in view, so a called shot at somebody's back still means getting behind them.

Open

How to show six facings of two defences without a spreadsheet on screen. Likely a ring around the unit, segmented, with shield as an outer band and armour as an inner one — readable at a glance, exact on hover. Six segments is probably too fine; three arcs of front, flanks and rear may carry the same decision at a fraction of the noise. The spread now has to be shown as well, since a shot is never at one plate: the sandbox lists all of them with their shares, which is honest and much too wordy.

11Built

Judged

What an action is worth, and what to do about it

Every decision in this game is the same decision: several things you could do, one purse to do them out of, and no way to tell which is better without a common scale. Until now there was no scale. The reaction window picked what to do off a ladder — shoot if you can, otherwise turn to face them, otherwise get low, otherwise call it in — which got the obvious case right and had nothing whatever to say about the interesting ones.

The scale is vitality

Not an abstract number between nought and one. A score answers how many points of soldier is this worth, and everything that is not vitality is converted into vitality so that it can be compared with something that is. That choice is forced by where the scorer has to work: inside a reaction window the currency is already action points, and an action point is already a tick of the mover’s timeline. A ranking that could not price a point against a wound would have nothing to say about the one decision a window actually poses — a cheap poor shot now against a good slow one that arrives after they reach cover.

TermWhat it isWhere it comes from
HarmVitality off them, nowThe shot, put through the layers it will actually meet
SparedVitality kept on usThe shot coming back, asked twice — once about the soldier as they are, once as they would be
ProspectVitality this sets upThe share of a look a facing buys, or the shot you hand somebody by calling it in

They are kept apart rather than summed away because an interface has to be able to say why. “He shoots because the shot is worth four and the dive is worth one” is a sentence a player can argue with; “he shoots because 4.1 > 1.2” is not. It is also the honest way to keep one query surface: if a term cannot be shown to a player it should not be in there, and if it can be shown then both sides have it.

What a shot is worth is not what a shot delivers

The single most important thing the old ladder could not see. Expected damage is worked out before shields and plate, so eight points of beam landing squarely on a full force shield reads beautifully and achieves precisely nothing — and the ladder would take that shot over anything else on offer, every time, because shots outranked postures and damage arriving was how shots were ranked.

Now the rounds are put through the layers they are going to meet, face by face, in order, against layers that wear as they go. That matters more than it sounds: a repeater round against a fresh face is stopped entirely, one point into the shield and five into the plate, and the plate is only eight thick — so the second round of the burst walks through the hole the first one made and the third walks through unopposed. A model that priced each round against untouched armour would rate the whole burst at three times nothing.

Three things come back rather than one, because they are worth different amounts. Vitality is the only one that wins the fight. Ablative plate stripped never comes back, so it is progress banked — and counting it is what stops the scorer rating the opening exchange of every firefight at nearly zero and preferring almost any posture to shooting. Shield soaked comes back in a turn or two, so it is worth very little, and what keeps it above nothing is the ambush: inside one window the second shot really does walk through the hole the first made, and a scorer that valued the first at nothing would never set that up.

Posture is derived, not ranked

Nothing says turning is better than dropping, or the other way round. Both are worked out by asking the ordinary questions twice, once about the soldier as they are and once about the soldier as they would be, and the geometry answers differently every time.

Turning earns almost nothing from armour. Every face starts with the same plate and the same shield, and the glancing average is deliberately flattened so that the bearing decides which side wears and not how much gets through — so on an unworn soldier, which way round they stand changes nothing at all about the round they catch. What turning buys is seeing them. A facing is worth the share of a look it gains times what you would do with what you saw, because you cannot shoot what you have not noticed. Staring harder at somebody you already hold a live fix on buys nothing, and the scoring says so by scaling on how far short of that fix the contact still is.

Getting low earns its keep twice over, and the two halves are not the same half. Cover and a smaller silhouette come through the shot itself, which is worth less against somebody hard to hit. Being harder to find is separate: a concealed silhouette divides what a look is worth, so flat in the open is exactly as easy to hit as upright and half as likely to be the thing anybody is aiming at. On bare ground that second half is the only one that moves — which is precisely right, and is why going flat in the open is worth doing and not worth much.

The result nobody wrote down: cover works in both directions and the score knows it. Diving flat behind a knee-high wall takes you out of sight of the man in front of you and takes him out of yours, so the appraisal comes back with what you saved and, alongside it, a negative figure for the shot you gave up to save it. That fell out of asking the same question about the same geometry from the other end. It was not a rule anybody added.

Firing is the loudest thing a soldier does

A shot is not worth its damage. In a game whose whole first act is about not being found, pulling a trigger is the most expensive thing you can do with a point: a slug rifle is heard through walls for eighteen metres, a beam paints a bright line straight back down its own path, and the man you shot at knows for certain that somebody is out there whatever he could make out a moment before. All three channels already existed; nothing could ask about them without firing.

So the score of a shot carries a second term, and it is the exact inverse of what calling a contact in is worth. For everybody the shot carries closer to acting: how much closer, times what they would then do about you. Somebody who already has a live fix learns nothing and costs nothing. Somebody who had no idea you existed learns the most and costs the most. It sits in the same place as diving into cover, because it is the same quantity with the sign reversed — vitality that will not be staying on us.

The one thing that runs out

Everything else a soldier spends comes back. Points refill every turn, shields recharge, and even a plate worn through is only ever worn through once by the man wearing it. A charge thrown is gone, and that is a kind of cost the scorer had no way to express: the first version of it, given two grenades and an enemy, threw both at the first soldier it saw. It was not wrong about what a grenade does. It was wrong about what it costs.

So a charge is worth something while it is still on the belt, in the same currency as everything else, and spending one pays that as well as the points. Set at about what one clean rifle shot achieves, it draws exactly the line the design wants and no rule states: a grenade is for the shot you cannot take. Against a soldier standing in the open a rifle is nearly as good and costs nothing, so the charge stays on the belt; against one behind a wall the rifle is worth nothing at all, so it comes off. What it does not yet model is that the last one should be dearer than the first, and that a charge carried out of the battle was worth nothing — both need a notion of how much fight is left, which nothing in the rules has.

A blast is also the first action in the game that can be worth a negative number because of who else was standing there. It catches your own side as readily as theirs, so the score sums over everybody in the radius and signs each by whose they are. A commander will therefore decline a grenade that would catch its own squad. It will not go looking for the throw that avoids them: offsetting the landing point is a search — every node in range crossed with everybody in the blast — and it is the same shape of limit as everything else a one-step search cannot see.

One scorer, two searches

The decision worth recording, because it is the one most likely to be made by accident. What varies between picking a reaction and taking a turn is which candidates get generated and how they chain together — not what any one of them is worth. A snap shot at somebody crossing your arc is worth what it is worth whether the points come out of a reserve or an allowance. So the arithmetic lives in one place and is called once per candidate, and the search lives with whatever is doing the searching.

The constraint that shaped everything: the scorer reads only what its soldier knows. Threats come from that unit’s own contact file rather than from the field, so an AI cannot stand well against a flanker it has never seen, and what the enemy holds on it is read at the rung the player is shown — it was read as the number for two increments, and the interface audit found it rather than Core. That is the same constraint the interface works under, and holding to it is what produced most of the new code — the armour-aware forecast, the look that has not been taken yet, the shot a threat could take if it had the points, what a shot would tell everybody. Every one of them is a figure a player should have been able to see and could not.

The search: greedy, with one step of lookahead on moves

Each pass picks the best single action, does it, and looks again from where that left the soldier. The candidates are everywhere it could stand crossed with everybody it could shoot from there, plus turning, getting lower, and declaring an arc. Not optimal play and not meant to be — a soldier deciding what to do next from what it can currently see is a fair model of a soldier, and searching whole turns costs combinatorially more for a game whose numbers have never been measured against anything.

The lookahead exists for one reason: walking is not an achievement. A move to a firing position is worth nothing on its own — it costs points and leaves you standing somewhere marginally worse — and it is worth a great deal because of the shot at the far end of it. Rank a move on itself and no unit ever takes a firing position; fold the shot into the move’s own appraisal and you have claimed the walk did something it did not. So they are carried separately, and what falls out is a soldier that goes round a man in cover rather than shooting through it — nobody wrote that down, the sight trace reports less of a target behind sandbags and the arithmetic does the rest.

Doing nothing is a candidate with a real score. Points left at the end of a turn become a reserve, and the reserve is what you answer somebody else's move with, so holding is worth whatever it could afford to do about the threats you know about. That has a cliff in it and the cliff is the point: below the floor a leftover banks nothing at all, and a bank that cannot afford the cheapest way of firing is worth nothing to shoot with either. An action has to beat holding rather than merely beat zero, which is what stops a soldier shuffling all turn for a tenth of a point and arriving at the end of it with nothing to answer anybody with. It is also why declaring an arc is ever chosen: all of an overwatch's value is in the reserve it leaves and the bonus that reserve now shoots at, so which arc to declare comes out of the same arithmetic as everything else rather than off a preference list.

Going to look

A threat is a belief, and the scorer says how good a belief it is. A contact the soldier looked at last and can still see stands where it really is. Anything else stands at the marker — where contact was last made, upright, because the question a marker answers is whether the place can be seen — and counts for a credence: all of a sighting for the two rounds in which the man may not have had a turn to leave, then half as much per round, because a soldier who stayed put through one turn is about as likely as not to stay through the next. That is a separate dial from the awareness ladder and it does a separate job: the ladder says how sure you are that somebody is out there, the credence says how sure you are that they are still there, and it is the second that stops a unit chasing ghosts round the map.

Nothing new was added to the search to make a unit hunt. A marker in view from the far end of a walk is a place a shot could be taken at, and the same one-step lookahead that sends a soldier round a man in cover sends it round a corner it heard something behind, with the shot it opens counted at the credence. Two things had to change in the scorer before that walk was ever worth taking, and both were measured rather than argued: the look a marker made by ear would get at the arrival is averaged over every way the man could be facing — assuming he is looking straight at you priced the walk above the shot on every geometry tried — and a look that finds a marker is worth the whole shot, because what is missing about a man you cannot see is where he is, not whether he exists. A marker with a facing — a briefed post, or a man seen and then lost — is priced with it, because half a look is the wrong price for both the road he watches and the ground behind him.

The commander never fires at a marker. A shot is offered only at a threat in view. The battle resolves a shot against where the target really is, so firing at a guess would have the battle correcting the soldier’s aim for free. A unit walks to where it can see the marker, looks at the end of its turn like everybody else, and shoots on the next one — and the man it found looks at the end of his turn too, so whichever of them comes round first fires first. Going to look costs a turn, and that turn is the window section 07 says the found soldier is owed.

The walk itself is priced now, too. A route’s loudness used to be worked out after the move and thrown away; it is a public query, with who would hear it beside it, and a move’s score carries what the walk tells everybody exactly as a shot’s carries what the trigger does. Going to look is worth less when the going gives you away, which is the argument for the long quiet way round.

Measured on a solid wall with three a side either side of it and one loud noise: five hexes long and the far side is found and the fight happens; eleven and nobody moves. Hunting reaches one move. Past about five hexes the walk costs more than the one discounted shot it might open, and a search one step deep cannot see the turn of shooting beyond it — the blade-carrier limit again. The same limit made two survivors who lost contact out of reach of each other a stalemate: markers decay, nobody walks. So the test that beliefs work asserts a fight and does not promise a decision. A soldier with an objective is not subject to that limit, because an objective slopes and a marker does not: it is worth walking toward from anywhere within about four turns of it. Hunting still reaches one move.

Something to want

The largest hole in this scorer was that a unit which knew about nobody scored every option at nothing and banked its turn. That was correct rather than convenient — there was genuinely nothing in the game for it to want — and it is why a fight that loses contact used to be a stalemate. It is also not a search-depth problem: a deeper search would have had nothing to search for.

An objective fills it with one multiplication. The objective says how much of itself a place represents, from nothing to all of it; the scorer says what all of it is worth. Nothing about which mission it is reaches the arithmetic, which is what keeps a win condition from quietly becoming a scoring model — the campaign grades casualties and the tactics layer must not be made to disagree with it about what a soldier is worth.

The part that makes it work is that progress is a gradient and not a flag. Worth everything from inside the exit and nothing one pace outside it, and a search one step deep would never set off: it cannot see a place three turns away. Sloped over the approach, every stride toward it scores, which is the same trick that makes a move to a firing position worth taking. The slope is measured in action points along the movement graph rather than in metres across the map, so the far side of a river reads as far away even where the water is narrow, and it is one search backwards from the objective rather than one per candidate destination.

It goes in the term that carries what an action sets up rather than what it delivers, and it is not discounted the way a posture is. A posture is a bet on next round; the mission is the reason the squad is on the map, and the future it belongs to is the one after the battle. So it is deliberately larger than anything a shot can score: a squad told to get out and not be seen walks out through fire rather than stopping to trade, which is the behaviour the whole complaint about elimination was asking for.

A mission has something in the middle of it

The first version modelled only the leaving, and twelve matches on a real map found the hole in round two of every one of them: the brief said enter the compound, confirm what is stored in the house, and come out, the rules judged the coming out, and the exit was one turn away while the compound was two turns past it in the other direction. So the best available play was to walk out of the gate immediately, and the AI played it — correctly, given what it had been told. Every shape in the mission book has something to do before the leaving, and only the leaving was there.

The fix is not another term. A mission is one journey and it is measured in action points: getting to the place, doing whatever is there, and getting to the exit. Twenty points spent walking and twenty spent on a charge move the same quantity by the same amount, so a two-stage mission needs no second scale and no seam — finishing the task does not jolt the score, it shortens what is left. And the exit stops being the far end: standing on it with the job undone is further from done than standing at the place, because the way home runs by way of the thing you came for.

That leaves one shape and three tasks. What they share is nearly everything; what differs is a single question, has the thing been done. Looking at a place is a geometric fact checked at the moment somebody looks — which is the end of their own turn, the only moment anybody sees anything here. Spending points on a charge is an action with a verb and a price. Withdrawal is the degenerate one, with nothing in the middle.

The horizon stretches to the length of the job. A mission longer than the few turns the slope was set at would read as nothing worth starting from anywhere near the start, which is a flag again with extra steps — so the horizon is the larger of the configured one and the journey the squad actually has in front of it, measured off the deployment. It decides how steep the slope is on a short mission, never how far it reaches.

And the AI is not offered the chance to walk out with the job undone. The rules allow it — abandoning is a real decision, and it is one of the three endings — but weighing cut our losses against press on means knowing how the whole battle is going, and a scorer that ranks one action against another at one moment does not. That is now the sharpest thing this search cannot do, and it arrived with objectives rather than being fixed by them.

What ends the task short

Every briefing in the book has six parts and the sixth is when it stops. Until now that part had nowhere to go: a mission file could say rounds 30 and the rules never read it, so whatever happened to be driving the battle applied the limit and two things driving it could apply different ones.

It is two halves and the whole of the design is putting them in different places. A round limit is a property of the mission. The same ground with the same garrison carries a different clock under a different briefing, and there is nothing about a waystation that says an inspection has to be over before first light — so it hangs on the objective, where a mission file can set it, and it is a round number and a comparison. The alarm going out is a fact about the awareness model. Somebody carrying a set worked out that there is a person here, and passed it on; that is true of this ground with no mission on it at all, and it is produced by the same relay that already carries a contact side-wide. Putting the fact on the mission would have every future mission shape re-deriving it from contacts; putting the limit in the awareness model would have the awareness model holding a number that belongs to a briefing.

A set, not a shout. Word travels three ways here — a radio reaches the whole side, a voice reaches fifteen metres, and seeing a comrade react reaches as far as you can see — and only the first is an alarm. That distinction is load-bearing on real ground rather than decorative: on the waystation the barn and the tower are both outside earshot of the compound, so a sentry who shouts has told one man and a sentry with a set has told the garrison.

And word already sent cannot be unsent. Everything a departing soldier knew is forgotten the moment they leave, which is what makes silencing a witness work with no rule saying so; an alarm they raised first is not. A squad that kills the signaller one second too late has not bought the second back. That asymmetry is the whole reason the set is worth killing first rather than merely killing, and it is the counter-play to the same forgetting that makes the quiet kill good.

The clock running out is abandonment, not failure. A squad still in the field when its hour passes has lost the mission and has not lost the squad, which is exactly the distinction the third ending was added for; failure stays reserved for there being nobody left who could have brought the answer home.

Not being seen, priced

The first measured numbers said what was missing, in one line: nothing wins the waystation, and the reason is not a dial. What an objective is worth paid for getting to the place and getting home; the rung the mission is judged on appeared nowhere in the arithmetic. So a reconnaissance was scored as a race, every commander ran it, and every squad was held at Engaged before it got its look.

The term is one more share of the objective. For each enemy, what they will hold on this soldier after the action is read as a share of the way to the rung the mission is lost at, and the worst of them is how much of the mission is gone. What the action is worth is the difference between that reading for the pose after and the pose before, times what the objective is worth. It goes with the objective's own term, undiscounted, because it is the mission and not a bet on next round. Nothing about which mission it is reaches the arithmetic: the objective says which rung it tolerates, and a mission that tolerates the top of the ladder prices being seen at nothing.

Four things go into what each enemy will hold, and the transcript of one briefed match put two of them there. What they hold now, read as the rung and never the number. The look a walk hands them now: a reaction window gives anybody with reserve one look at the first step of a route it can see that is not behind it, and the term prices that look exactly as the window takes it, so a walk across a sentry's front to a hiding place is scored on the crossing and not on the hiding place — before it was, the followers on the waystation were tagged in transit by the gate sentry on the way to ground the term had priced as hidden. Then the look the arrival hands them on their next turn, or a round of forgetting if it finds nothing, as the awareness model does — a look a round for as long as the pose is held, or forgetting for as long as it is not, counted over the rounds until the job is done or the night is out and discounted geometrically at the scorer's one look-ahead dial, because being in view is a rate, and a look under the bar that a round of hiding takes back is not a quarter of the mission. And whatever the noise hands them, if there is noise.

A slope up to the rung, not a cliff at it, and that was the choice to make deliberately. The win condition is a cliff. But a departure reading is reached by accumulation — a look a turn, a noise a move — and a search one step deep priced against the cliff alone would walk a soldier to one point short of it and then find that every remaining move, and the sentry's own next look, costs the whole mission. So what is priced is how far up the ladder somebody is, which is the same curve the scorer already uses for how much closer to acting a listener gets. It is the argument for gradients over flags a third time.

And the slope carries on past the rung, up to the ceiling. The first version clamped it at the rung, on the briefing's own line — if the shift sees you the task is over — so that going loud was free once you were seen. The transcript said what that did. The mission is judged at departure and a contact decays, so a soldier held at Searching who is out of sight for a round is held at Suspicious and the mission is whole again; clamped, the term read every pose as the same nought the moment anybody held a soldier at the rung, so staying in the eye, stepping out of it and firing all cost nothing more, and the followers walked to the gate the turn they were noticed. Past the rung the share is how many rounds of hiding the recovery would take, stepping out of the eye is worth one of them, and a shot at the man who already has you costs what it hands everybody else and no more.

Two consequences fall out of the shape rather than being written down. A soldier's own reading rather than the squad's worst means each keeps itself quiet even after a comrade is noticed — a contact decays and a witness can be silenced, and a squad that stopped being careful the moment one of its own was seen would throw the recovery away. Entry 087 asked whether that choice was what set the followers fighting; the transcript said it was the clamp and the crossing, and the choice stands. And a shot is priced with the man being shot at counted only at the chance he survives it, because the battle forgets everything a departed soldier held: that is the whole argument for the quiet kill, and the model makes it.

What an enemy holds is read as the rung and never the number, for the same reason the rest of the scorer reads it that way. What they will hold is read against everybody the soldier has registered at all, down to a suspicion — a place you half-glimpsed something in is a place to keep out of the eye of, well before it is somebody to fire on — and that list is wider than the one a soldier would shoot at, on purpose.

What a briefing is

A side can be briefed before the fight: every soldier of it is handed a marker on an enemy at the post he is deployed to, at the rung the mission says, facing the way it says he watches. Entry 087 asked whether a briefing should be a place to keep out of the eye of, a threat to hunt, or a marker that starts stale. It is the first two at once, and that needed no rule: the fight terms hunt a briefed marker at full credence, and the quiet term outweighs them for as long as it is alive — the transcript's whole finding was the two ways it had been going dead. What did need a rule is that a post is not a sighting. A sighting decays because the man may have moved; a post is where he was put, and a soldier who has had no line to it has learned nothing by looking. So a briefed contact holds until the soldier has had a line to the place it names and found nobody there, or has learned of the man by another channel, and only then fades like any other. The first version decayed it like a sighting, and by round three the scout had forgotten the gate was manned.

A marker carries the facing it was given or seen with. Averaged over six facings, the road past the gate sentry reads as half a look; read with the facing the briefing states, it is a full look from the front and next to nothing from behind, which is the whole of an approach. A marker made by ear has no facing and keeps the average.

The hour, as the scorer sees it

The same batch measured three rounds of grace ending most matches with fewer than half the squad home, against a squad that did not know it had three rounds. A per-action scorer cannot price time: it weighs one action against another at one moment, and this turn is one fewer is not a property of any action. What it can see is a rate, so the clock goes into the one rate that matters. The objective's slope is stretched by the ratio of the mission's length to the night left, in the same action points: one while the night is at least as long as the mission, steeper once it is not, and every stride toward home worth a larger share than it was. The night is read live, because the alarm half of a deadline is not known until it happens.

Stretched rather than re-sloped over the night, and that is the whole of the decision. A slope that ran from all of it at the end to nothing as far off as there is night left would be flat beyond that, and a soldier further from home than the night allows would stop wanting home at all — which is the judgement the mission is lost, get out, and nothing here has it. Stretching keeps every stride worth something however late it is, and lets the whole read as worth more than the objective, which is fine: the objective's terms are only ever compared with what else a soldier could do, and a mission it cannot bring home in time is the one nothing on the field is worth trading for.

Open

Being come looking for costs the shooter nothing. What giving yourself away is worth is measured by what the listener could do from where they stand, and from behind a wall that is nothing. A unit now does come looking, so the figure is wrong; the right one is the best shot the listener could reach in a turn, which is a search inside a score, run inside every shot the scorer weighs. Calling a contact in to an ally with no line has the same gap from the other side.

The marker’s decay is the second dial to watch. At a half per round a marker three rounds old is worth a quarter of a sighting and fades to nothing in five. Too quick to hunt with, or too slow to stop chasing ghosts, is a question a batch of matches answers, and the batch can run now.

Briefed, nobody is seen and nobody goes in; blind, the squad wins one in seven by waiting to be forgotten. Measured. With the crossing priced as the window takes it, the slope carried past the rung and the briefing holding, a briefed squad finds the hidden ground at the bridge in round one and stays there all night: not a shot, not an alarm, not a look in a hundred matches, because every way forward crosses the gate sentry's front or is heard by four men, and the way round leaves the direct line before it comes back, which a search one step deep cannot see. Blind, the same squad walks in and is seen as before, then hides until the contacts decay and walks out under Suspicious — fifteen matches of a hundred achieved where there were none, in thirteen rounds where there were four. Two things follow. The objective's pull has to run the quiet way round, which is the next step. And being forgotten is now the second best move in the game after the quiet kill, on a decay dial nobody has measured.

What an objective is worth is saturated, twice over. Measured. Sixty, a hundred and twenty and two hundred and forty are one row with the quiet term in as they were without it, because the term is a share of the objective and raising the value raises both sides of the trade at once. The live range is thirty to sixty and nothing in it wins the waystation. Also measured: the horizon is the same dial. On a journey longer than it, it is stretched and does nothing; past the journey, the scorer answers to value per point of ground.

Removing a signaller is worth no more than removing the rifleman beside them, and turning the bonus up does not change that. Measured. The first shot goes at the sentry on the road four times in five at every setting tried: the bonus turns wounds into kills and cannot say that one first. Weighting removal by what a soldier does for its side is still the obvious next thing and is still not derived from anything — and on the waystation the word is out in round two, before anybody could act on it.

A shot that kills its target still gives you away to the target, in the fight term. The mission term counts him only at the chance he survives, which is the quiet kill priced; what he could do about you is still priced as if he will. And the same preview leaves out what the target passes on to his friends, which is the relay that raises the alarm — found by the interface, and now with two consumers.

A blade carrier will not cross open ground to reach you. One step of lookahead cannot see a knife going in two turns from now, so it correctly concludes that this turn's walk into rifle range is worse than standing still — and then stands still forever. That is a limit of the search rather than a fault in the scoring, and it is the first thing a deeper one would fix.

Most rates here are still arguments. Four have been measured and the rest have not; the instrument is checked in beside the tests, off by default, and takes the same seeds every time. The one to point it at next, once the missing term exists, is what a soaked shield is worth: at the figure set today a fully soaked beam scores near enough exactly what the shot costs, so the ordering between firing pointlessly and doing something else is decided by noise.
